The z-Transform and Discrete-Time Systems

A difference equation is the discrete cousin of a differential equation. The z-transform turns it into a rational function, and the unit circle then answers every stability and frequency question at once.

  • Transform the standard discrete sequences and use the shift property on difference equations.
  • Explain why the ROC is an annulus and how it relates to causality and stability.
  • Invert a rational z-transform by partial fractions and by long division.
  • Read the impulse response and the frequency response from the pole–zero pattern.
  • Explain how the DFT samples the z-transform on the unit circle.
Start with a question

A digital filter is described by three coefficients: 1, −1.6 and 0.64. Its sampling rate is 8 kHz. Without plotting anything, can you say whether it rings, how fast it rings, and whether it is stable? The three numbers are enough — if you know where to look.

Section 7.5 puts those coefficients on the z-plane and answers all three questions from the pole radius and angle.

7.1 Why discrete systems need their own transform

A discrete-time LTI system is described by a difference equation, such as y[n] = x[n] − 0.5y[n−1]. The output depends on the present input and on past outputs. Iterating the equation gives the impulse response; the z-transform gives it in closed form.

Two-sided z-transformCORE
X(z) = Σn=−∞∞ x[n]z−n
  • z a complex variable, z = rejω
  • |z| = 1 the unit circle, where the transform becomes the DTFT
  • z−1 a delay of one sample

The transform is a power series in z−1. It converges where that series converges, which is an annulus centred on the origin.

The delay property is the workhorse. If x[n] ↔ X(z), then x[n−k] ↔ z−kX(z). Apply it to every term of a difference equation, solve for Y(z)/X(z), and the system function appears as a rational function of z.

The substitution z = ejω evaluates the transform on the unit circle and produces the discrete-time Fourier transform. The frequency response is therefore a slice through the z-plane, and the angle ω is the normalised frequency in radians per sample.

7.2 Pairs, properties and the meaning of z−1

Every z-domain expression should be read as a recipe for samples. A factor of z−1 is a one-sample delay; a pole at a is a geometric decay an; a zero at b is a subtraction of a delayed and scaled copy.

The shift property, linearity and the convolution property (convolution in n becomes multiplication in z) are enough for most problems. The initial-value theorem is simpler than in continuous time: x[0] = limz→∞X(z) when the transform is proper. The final-value theorem requires all poles of (z−1)X(z) to lie inside the unit circle.

7.3 The unit circle is the stability boundary

In the s-plane the stability boundary is the imaginary axis. Under the mapping z = esT that axis wraps onto the unit circle, the left half-plane maps inside the circle and the right half-plane maps outside it. Every stability statement transfers directly.

  • Stable: every pole strictly inside the unit circle, |pk| < 1.
  • Marginally stable: simple poles on the circle, |pk| = 1, and none outside.
  • Unstable: at least one pole outside the circle, |pk| > 1, or a repeated pole on the circle.

The ROC of a causal system is the exterior of the largest pole. If that region includes the unit circle, the system is stable. This is why a single glance at the pole radius answers the question: a pole at 0.9 decays, a pole at 1.1 grows, and a pole at exactly 1 oscillates forever or accumulates.

Second-order systems add one more visual cue: the pole angle sets the ringing frequency. A pole at radius r and angle θ produces a damped oscillation whose envelope decays as rn and whose period is 2π/θ samples.

7.4 From difference equation to difference equation

The round trip is the core skill. Given y[n] − 0.8y[n−1] = x[n], transform both sides: Y(z)(1 − 0.8z−1) = X(z), so H(z) = 1/(1 − 0.8z−1) = z/(z − 0.8). The impulse response is 0.8nu[n], and the pole at 0.8 is inside the unit circle, so the system is stable.

Going the other way, multiply numerator and denominator by powers of z until both are polynomials in positive powers. The coefficients of the denominator give the feedback coefficients of the recursion; the coefficients of the numerator give the feedforward coefficients. A direct-form implementation is literally a delay line with these coefficients.

Partial fractions invert a rational transform into a sum of geometric sequences. For distinct poles, H(z)/z is expanded first, then each term is matched to z/(z−p). Dividing by z before expanding is the standard trick because the numerator of every basic pair contains a factor z.

Worked example 7.1

Iterate, then verify in the z-domain

A system has y[n] = 0.7y[n−1] + x[n] and y[−1] = 0. Find y[3] for a unit impulse.

Iterate. y[0] = 0.7·0 + 1 = 1; y[1] = 0.7; y[2] = 0.49; y[3] = 0.343.

Closed form. h[n] = 0.7nu[n], so h[3] = 0.7³ = 0.343. The two routes agree.

Stability. The pole is at z = 0.7, inside the unit circle. The impulse response decays, the system is stable, and the DC gain is H(1) = 1/(1 − 0.7) = 3.333.

Worked example 7.2

Inverse z by partial fractions

Find the causal inverse of H(z) = z/(z² − 1.5z + 0.5).

Factor the denominator. z² − 1.5z + 0.5 = (z − 1)(z − 0.5). Both poles are on or inside the unit circle; the pole at 1 makes the system marginally stable, not stable.

Expand H(z)/z. 1/[(z−1)(z−0.5)] = A/(z−1) + B/(z−0.5). Cover z = 1: A = 1/0.5 = 2. Cover z = 0.5: B = 1/(0.5−1) = −2.

Reconstruct. H(z) = 2z/(z−1) − 2z/(z−0.5), so h[n] = 2(1 − 0.5n)u[n]. The response tends to 2, a constant, which is consistent with a pole at z = 1 and a DC gain of 2.

7.5 Frequency response and the DFT

Evaluating H(z) on the unit circle gives the frequency response. As the angle θ moves from 0 to π, the frequency moves from DC to the Nyquist frequency. A pole near the circle at angle θ₀ produces a resonant peak near that frequency; a zero on the circle produces a perfect null.

The discrete Fourier transform is the z-transform sampled at N equally spaced points on the unit circle: zk = ej2πk/N. This is why the DFT is periodic with period N and why frequency resolution improves with longer records. It also explains spectral leakage: a frequency that does not fall exactly on a sample point spreads energy across neighbouring bins.

The sampling rate sets the meaningful frequency range. Frequencies above half the sampling rate alias, so the digital filter can only shape the band from 0 to fs/2. A pole angle of π corresponds to fs/2; an angle of π/2 corresponds to fs/4.

7.8 Simpler explain

Simpler explain

In discrete time a system works sample by sample. The z-transform is a bookkeeping trick that turns the endless chain of "multiply the previous output by a and add the new input" into a single fraction.

The fraction's denominator has roots called poles. A pole inside the unit circle means the effect of an old sample fades away. A pole outside means it grows. A pole exactly on the circle means it never fades — a permanent ring or a constant offset.

The angle of the pole tells you the pitch of the ring: small angles are slow wiggles, angles near 180° are fast alternations. The radius tells you how long the ring lasts. Digital filter design is largely the art of placing poles and zeros where you want those two effects.

Foundation check

For H(z) = z/(z − 0.9), h[n] = 0.9n. After 10 samples the response is 0.910 = 0.349, after 20 samples 0.122. A pole at 0.9 is stable but slow. Moving it to 0.5 makes the response decay to 0.001 after 10 samples — faster, but with a wider bandwidth.

Deep dive

The z-transform and the Laplace transform are related by z = esT. The left half-plane maps inside the unit circle, the imaginary axis maps onto the circle, and horizontal strips of width 2π/T map onto the whole plane repeatedly. This many-to-one mapping is exactly aliasing in the frequency domain: different continuous frequencies that differ by multiples of the sampling rate land on the same z. Understanding the map makes the sampling theorem in Module 8 feel inevitable rather than arbitrary.
