Laplace Transform and Continuous-Time Systems

The Laplace transform turns differential equations into algebra and draws a system's entire personality on one complex plane. Poles tell you how fast it responds, whether it rings and whether it survives.

  • Transform and invert the standard signals and explain the role of the region of convergence.
  • Use the initial- and final-value theorems as checks on a solution.
  • Resolve a rational transform into partial fractions and invert it.
  • Read damping, natural frequency and stability from pole locations.
  • Compute a transfer function from a circuit or an ODE and use it to predict the step response.
Start with a question

Two circuits have the same DC gain and the same cutoff frequency. One overshoots its step response by 20%; the other never overshoots at all. Their transfer functions differ in only one number. Which number is it, and where does it sit on the s-plane?

Section 6.5 answers this by reading the poles. By section 6.6 you will predict the overshoot before plotting anything.

6.1 From the Fourier axis to the whole plane

The Fourier transform evaluates a signal against everlasting sinusoids ejωt. Many useful signals — a growing exponential, a step that never ends, an unstable oscillator — do not have a Fourier transform in the ordinary sense. The Laplace transform fixes this by allowing the test exponential to grow or decay:

Two-sided Laplace transformCORE
X(s) = ∫−∞∞ x(t)e−stdt,   s = σ + jω
  • σ the real part, controlling growth or decay
  • ω the imaginary part, controlling oscillation
  • s a complex frequency

Setting σ = 0 recovers the Fourier transform along the imaginary axis. The Laplace transform is therefore a Fourier transform evaluated on a vertical line, extended over the whole plane.

The price of the extension is the region of convergence (ROC): the set of s values for which the integral converges. A transform without its ROC is incomplete, because the same algebraic expression can correspond to different time signals depending on which side of the poles the ROC lies.

Initial and final value theorems are checks, not shortcuts

The initial-value theorem states f(0⁺) = lims→∞ sF(s). The final-value theorem states f(∞) = lims→0 sF(s), but only if every pole of sF(s) is in the left half-plane. Applying the final-value theorem to an unstable system returns a finite number that has nothing to do with the actual response. Check stability first.

6.3 Reading the region of convergence

The ROC is a vertical strip or half-plane in the s-plane. For a right-sided signal it lies to the right of the rightmost pole; for a left-sided signal, to the left of the leftmost pole; for a two-sided signal, in the strip between two poles. The boundaries are always vertical lines through the poles, because convergence depends only on the real part σ.

Three consequences matter for systems:

  • Causality requires a right-sided ROC that extends to infinity.
  • Stability requires the ROC to include the imaginary axis. If the jω axis is inside the ROC, the Fourier transform exists and every bounded input gives a bounded output.
  • Causal and stable simultaneously requires every pole to lie strictly in the left half-plane.

A pole exactly on the imaginary axis produces a sustained oscillation or a constant: the system is marginally stable, not stable. A double pole on the axis produces a growing ramp.

6.4 Inverse transforms by partial fractions

A rational transform with a higher-degree denominator can be split into first-order terms whose inverses are known. For distinct poles:

Partial-fraction expansionMETHOD
F(s) = A₁/(s + p₁) + A₂/(s + p₂) + …  ⟹  f(t) = Σ Ake−pktu(t)
  • Ak the residue at pole pk
  • pk a pole location
  • right-sided the assumed ROC for a causal system

Repeated poles produce terms tme−pt. Complex poles produce damped sinusoids; pair the conjugates to avoid complex arithmetic in the final answer.

Residues are computed by covering the pole and evaluating the rest: for a simple pole at s = −pk, multiply both sides by (s + pk) and set s = −pk. The sum of the residues equals the coefficient of the highest-power term, which is a useful check when the numerator and denominator have the same degree.

Worked example 6.1

Invert a second-order transform

Find y(t) for Y(s) = 12/[(s + 1)(s + 3)], causal.

Split. 12/[(s+1)(s+3)] = A/(s+1) + B/(s+3). Cover s = −1: A = 12/(−1+3) = 6. Cover s = −3: B = 12/(−3+1) = −6.

Invert. y(t) = 6e−tu(t) − 6e−3tu(t).

Check. y(0⁺) = 6 − 6 = 0, which matches the initial-value theorem: lim sY(s) as s→∞ = 0. y(∞) = 0, which matches the final-value theorem because both poles are in the left half-plane. The response rises, peaks, then decays as the slower exponential dominates.

6.5 Poles, zeros and the shape of the response

Write a transfer function in factored form, H(s) = K(s − z₁)(s − z₂)…/[(s − p₁)(s − p₂)…]. The poles pk are the natural frequencies of the system; the zeros zk are the frequencies at which the response is blocked.

A real pole at s = −a contributes a decaying exponential with time constant 1/a. The farther left the pole, the faster the decay. A complex pair at σ ± jωd contributes eσtcos(ωdt + φ): the real part sets the decay envelope, the imaginary part sets the ringing frequency.

A zero near the imaginary axis can slow the rise or create overshoot by differentiating the response. A zero in the right half-plane produces inverse response: the output initially moves in the opposite direction before recovering. Such non-minimum-phase zeros are common in aircraft and chemical processes and are a genuine control challenge.

A pole at the origin is a pure integrator: a step input produces a ramp. A pole on the imaginary axis produces a sustained sinusoid. A pole in the right half-plane produces growth, which is why the right half-plane is the instability region.

Worked example 6.2

Damping from a denominator

A second-order system has H(s) = 25/(s² + 6s + 25). Find the natural frequency, the damping ratio, the damped frequency and the overshoot.

Standard form. Compare with s² + 2ζω_n s + ω_n². Here ω_n² = 25, so ω_n = 5 rad/s; 2ζω_n = 6, so ζ = 6/10 = 0.6.

Damped frequency. ω_d = ω_n√(1 − ζ²) = 5√(1 − 0.36) = 5 × 0.8 = 4 rad/s.

Poles. s = −3 ± j4. Both have negative real parts, so the system is stable.

Overshoot. M_p = e−πζ/√(1−ζ²) = e−π(0.6)/0.8 = e−2.356 = 0.0946, about 9.5%. The step response settles to 1 with a ringing frequency of 4 rad/s.

6.8 Simpler explain

Simpler explain

The Laplace transform is a machine for turning calculus into algebra. A differential equation becomes a fraction in s. You simplify the fraction, look up the pieces in a table, and write down the time response.

The denominator's roots — the poles — tell you how the system behaves. A pole far to the left means a fast decay. A pole near the imaginary axis means slow decay or ringing. A pole on the right means the system is running away. A pair of poles above and below the axis means oscillation.

The zeros shape the response but do not create it. They can make a system overshoot or start in the wrong direction, but they do not decide stability. Stability is a property of the poles alone.

Foundation check

For H(s) = 1/(s + 2), the pole is at −2, the time constant is 0.5 s, and the step response is ½(1 − e−2t). At t = 0.5 s the response has reached 63% of its final value. That 63% point is the definition of one time constant.

Deep dive

The Laplace transform is the eigenbasis of differentiation: d/dt ↔ s. That is why constant-coefficient ODEs become polynomials. The ROC exists because the eigenfunctions are not normalisable; the transform converges only where the exponential decay of e−σt beats the growth of the signal. In the z-domain the same story unfolds with z = esT, and the imaginary axis maps to the unit circle.
